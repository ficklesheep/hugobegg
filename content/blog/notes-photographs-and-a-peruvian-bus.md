There are beautiful parallels between photography and music. Light and shadow meet loud and soft; frames both cluttered and minimal meet music's commotion and tranquility. There's a fine balance between colours, textures, lines, and contours, which both mediums put forth so differently to the world. Differently, yet so vastly alike. As music commands the ears, photos command the eyes — both masters of their own sense. The way music paints a picture equals that of a photograph's own harmonies. What can't be said is spoken so eloquently. 

I am very privileged to have grown up in a musical family. I can't remember a time where there hasn't been the sound of scales, exercises and gorgeous etudes filling every corner of the house and my own head. A constant but welcome presence. In what would be a terrific origin story for a successful musician, my earliest musical memories are of playing inside my mother's velvet-lined guitar case, listening to her practise. I remember the soft fibers between my fingers, the musty smell, and the glorious sound of Bach preludes resonating the weatherboards in our house in Brisbane. I would carefully arrange toy cars on the busily patterned carpet on the floor at her cello lessons, and my sister's piano lessons saw me gleefully scooting around on my back over the slippery tiles. 

I would waste away warm Sunday afternoons playing in the front yard, hearing the lazy coos of crested pigeons and smelling the warm buffalo grass, the neighbourhood awash with the sounds of my family's many instruments. Playtime was always accompanied by music.

I'm sure the neighborhood was thrilled when my own small hands took command of the violin. I can only say I'm surprised at their patience when said violin suffered a lively, end-pin related death, my prayers were answered, and I migrated loudly to the trumpet. Wonderfully, this avenue of my creative pursuits is still open some 16 years later. I owe this to my ever-supportive family and my own patience as I progressed from raspberries to orchestral performances. I often think that without music, my love of photography may not have come about. Perhaps by also being constantly overshadowed by my sister's musical dedication and my own aversions to structured practice pushed me towards a different creative outlet.

A family trip to Japan in 2017 and my mum's old Pentax K100D were more than enough to spark a financially crippling interest in cameras, and a few years later, I purchased my own, and my endless storage problems quickly ensued.

My personal style has changed considerably over the years as I found my own feet amongst the sea of Instagram photographers and brand-ambassador wannabes constantly being shoved in front of my impressionable eyes. I chased editing trends, gear set-ups, and subject matter as I slowly discovered what truly interested me. I began to admire works for their circumstance and process, raw emotion and story, and their harmonies and dissonance – camera model-numbers, photographic rules, and advanced editing tools became irrelevant, and my photos started to become my own. I had a new medium for self-expression, a partner to my knowledge of music. Lines and contours became contours and lines, harmonies and melodies became colours and light, and the world became suddenly more beautiful.

Like my ability to share recent work, this blog will be sporadic. As inspired as I am writing this and as I have been these last few weeks, photographing the south of Peru, inspiration only comes and goes. I cannot claim to be a writer – my sister takes the cake on that one, too – I can only hope that whatever I do write sheds some amount of light onto my creative journey, however haphazard it may be. It's not that I have anything profound to say, but I hope that what my photographs can't say, I can help to fill in the gaps.

...

*I write this on a gently swaying bus that winds slowly through southern Peru's arid and seemingly endless mountains towards Bolivia. The sun is warm on my left shoulder, a breeze blows through the small window, providing the only relief to the typical and almost unbearable warmth of the upper floor, and a man is selling electric green cannabis sports cream. His small, hip-mounted amplifier provides him with little additional volume but adds a dramatic amount of reverb to what I'm sure is a very convincing sales pitch. He gives out small samples to vaguely interested bus patrons, and now the bus smells strongly of Vicks VapoRub. By pure fortune of booking with one of Peru's cheapest and worst-rated bus companies, my 26-minute late arrival to the bus terminal was no hindrance to my eight-hour (six Peruvian hours) journey, and I now sit comfortably in my 160-degree reclining seat, calmly watching the world go by. A small bribe of 0.5 soles to the lazy bus ticket vendor expedites my 1.5 sol terminal fee ticket acquisition process, and I skip the ten-minute line to the teller. A wasted effort as the bus leaves 15 minutes later.*
